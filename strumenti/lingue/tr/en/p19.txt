v1311	In depth: what happens inside the computer
v1312	<b>In CAD the data is the geometry.</b> For the computer a line is a row in an archive: two points and a few attributes. The wall does not exist: there are four independent rows, and the fact that together they are "a wall" is known only to whoever looks at them.
v1313	The area of the wall is not written anywhere: to know it you have to measure and multiply. If the wall gets one metre longer, you have to move two lines and then remember to correct the section, the elevation and the bill of quantities, which are other drawings and other files.
v1314	<b>In BIM the data is the object, and the geometry is calculated.</b> The wall is an object with a few independent values, the parameters; everything else follows from formulas, like the cells of a spreadsheet:
v1315	If you change <i>L</i> from 5 to 6 metres, the program redoes the calculations in cascade: the shape, the area (18 m²), the volume (5.4 m³), the views and the schedules. It is a dependency graph: each value knows which others it depends on, and when one changes only those downstream are updated. The same goes for relations between objects: the window is hosted by the wall and cuts its volume, the slab rests on the wall, the room is bounded by the walls around it.
v1316	<b>And when the model leaves the software?</b> In the open IFC format the wall is written like this, in simplified form: an <code>IfcWall</code>, that is a wall and not a set of lines; its shape as an <code>IfcExtrudedAreaSolid</code>, a rectangular profile extruded by the height; its properties in sets such as <code>Pset_WallCommon</code>, for example whether it is load-bearing or external; and the openings as relations (<code>IfcRelVoidsElement</code>). Whoever receives the file finds a wall with its rules, not four lines to interpret.
v1317	<b>The consequences.</b> Quantities become reliable because they are calculated instead of measured; changes cost less because they propagate by themselves; inconsistencies between drawings disappear, because the drawings are views of the same data. In exchange, more discipline is needed at the start: choosing the types, parameters and rules well, because an error in the data spreads as fast as a correction.
v1318	Nobody really knows, and we distrust anyone who claims to. But some paths are already open, and BIM News reports their first steps. Here we line them up, separating what is already happening from what, for now, is only a question.

v1325	Will drawings still be needed?
v1326	If the site reads the model, the printed drawing could become one view among many, useful for signing and archiving more than for building. But signatures, responsibility and the legal value of documents still go through paper, or PDF, today.
v1327	Robots on site?
v1328	A precise model is exactly what a machine needs to set out, drill or install. How much and when robots will enter everyday sites, and not only large projects, remains to be seen.
v1329	And us?
v1330	If the machine draws, checks and calculates, designers are left with the hardest part: understanding the problem, choosing, being accountable for the choices. Perhaps the profession does not disappear: its centre of gravity shifts. Who knows.
